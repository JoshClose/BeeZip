This plan builds on [`PLAN.md`](PLAN.md) and [`M0.md`](M0.md)–[`M13.md`](M13.md) and keeps their
decisions, except M13's decision 11 ("no encryption in version 1"), which it fulfils (§14). On

every way of opening an archive that was offered — a password, a recovery key, public keys and
certificates — and the others named as later options: security keys, Windows Hello and the
PC's TPM. And the build planned so that as much of it as possible runs at once (§10).

These findings shape the plan:
- **Zip and 7z can't be made very secure; `.bhive` can.** Zip's AES leaves names, sizes and
  dates in the clear and stretches a password 1,000 times with PBKDF2-SHA1: one RTX 4090 tries
  18.9 million passwords a second. 7z authenticates nothing (AES-CBC and a CRC of the plain
  bytes) and its stretching needs no memory, so graphics cards run it well. `.bhive`'s header
  has a features field that every released reader refuses when it doesn't know a bit, so an
  encrypted `.bhive` can't be misread by BeeZip 0.8.
- **The design every recent format converged on fits `.bhive` closely:** a random file key;
  "slots", each of which unwraps it (one per password, key or device); a MAC over the header
  that also commits to the key; a key of its own for each unit of data; nothing about the
  contents left in the clear. age, Apple's AEA, LUKS2, Tink and OpenPGP v6 each have most of it.
- **A memory-hard password hash is what makes guessing slow.** At the default cost (Argon2id,
  1 GiB, 2 passes, 4 lanes; about 0.3 s here) one core of this machine tries about 1.1
  passwords a second, against 194 for 7-Zip's default, 396 for RAR5 and 4,220 to 16,750 for
  WinZip. Graphics cards widen the gap, since each guess needs its own gigabyte: roughly 100
  guesses a second per card at 1 GiB, where WinZip allows 18.9 million (the helper's estimate;
  no published figure exists).
- **Encryption costs little next to compression.** AES-256-GCM seals 13–14 GiB/s on one core
  here and ChaCha20-Poly1305 3.3–3.5; on 32 threads both reach the memory's limit, about
  25 GiB/s. Added to `.bhive`, in memory: under 5 % to compress at every level; to extract,
  under 1.5 % at Fast, 5–14 % at Balanced, under 3.3 % at High and Max. Cells stored as they
  are (photos, video, other archives) cost 27–35 % more to extract in memory, because sealing
  them whole adds a pass over memory that the copy already fills; sealing in 1 MiB pieces keeps
  that in the cache (decision 4), and a disk usually hides it anyway.
- **Windows does everything certificates need, silently until a person is needed** (on this
  machine, unelevated): RSA-OAEP and ECDH with software and TPM keys; a TPM key needs no
  certificate and unwraps in about 20 ms (ECDH P-256); Windows' HMAC key derivation, the one
  smart cards are known to support, computes exactly HKDF's first step, so one definition works
  with smart cards, TPM keys and key files alike. Two traps: ECDSA certificates (most EC
  certificates) can't do ECDH, and CNG's own HKDF is broken and can crash the process.
- **Every primitive fits a stdlib-only Python reader:** ChaCha20-Poly1305 and AES-256-GCM,
  Argon2id, X25519, P-256 and P-384, RSA-OAEP, ML-KEM-768 and X-Wing all pass their official
  vectors. A 1 GiB encrypted archive opens in about 1.7 minutes there with AES-256-GCM (1.2
  with ChaCha20-Poly1305); a password at the default cost takes about 1.5 minutes with a
  process per lane.
- **One generation of RustCrypto crates covers it all and matches our lock:** about 46 new
  crate names, no duplicate versions. The `age` crate (an older generation, 66 new crates),
  aws-lc-rs (a C and NASM build) and Mozilla's FIDO2 crate (needs admin on Windows) are not
  needed.

## Scope

- **Encryption for `.bhive`,** written into its specification (`docs/bhive.md` §12) as a
  feature of version 1. Zip and 7z keep their own encryption as it is after 0.8.4.
- **Eight ways to open an archive** ("slots", §4), any number per archive, any one enough:
  password, recovery key, public key (classic or post-quantum hybrid), RSA certificate, EC
  certificate (P-256, P-384), and FIDO2 security key. Smart cards, YubiKeys, the PC's TPM and
  Windows Hello are where the private key of a certificate or FIDO2 slot can live.
- **Keys of the user's own:** making, storing, backing up and sharing them; finding certificates
  in Windows' stores and in files.
- **Changing who can open an archive** without recompressing it.
- **Signing** (§11): proving who made an archive's contents and that they haven't changed, with
  a BeeZip signing key or a certificate, on encrypted and unencrypted `.bhive` alike.
- **Everywhere BeeZip runs:** the core, the commands, the CLI, the MCP server, the desktop app,
  and the Python reader.

## 1. What it protects against, and what it doesn't

**Protects:** an archive on a lost laptop or USB stick, in cloud storage, in email, on a shared
drive. Without a way in, nobody learns the names, sizes, dates, folder layout or contents of
the files, or can change any byte without BeeZip noticing.

**Shows, by design:**
- that the file is an encrypted `.bhive`, and its total size;
- every cell's stored size and its journal's size, from the cell blocks' heads: so the number
  of cells and roughly how big each is, and roughly the unpacked size. At level None, or for one
  file that doesn't compress, a file's size can be read from them;
- through those sizes, how well each cell compressed and what was stored once. Someone who
  chooses part of what goes into an archive and sees how big it comes out can learn about the
  rest, as with any compressed and encrypted file. Padding stays out of scope (§12);
- how many slots it has and of which kinds; for FIDO2 slots, the credential's id (stable, so
  two archives for the same security key can be linked). Certificate and TPM slots carry a
  4-byte tag that changes with every slot and can be checked only by someone who holds that
  certificate's public key (§4).

**Rollback.** A file added to in place keeps its earlier index blocks and footers, so cutting it
back to an earlier footer gives a valid earlier version, header MAC and signatures included.
BeeZip doesn't write such files yet; the format allows them (bhive.md §3).

**Doesn't protect against:**
- **Weak passwords.** Argon2id slows guessing; it can't save `summer2026` (§5).
- **A machine that is compromised while the archive is open** (malware, a keylogger).
- **Losing every way in.** There is no back door. That is what the recovery key is for.
- **Forgery by someone who can open it.** Anyone who can open an archive knows its file key and
  could make a different archive that opens the same ways. With one owner (a password and a
  recovery key) that doesn't matter; with public keys, anyone who has your public key can make
  an archive you can open. BeeZip says so when it matters (§5, decision 12). Signing is the cure
  (§11): an archive signed by its maker can't be passed off as theirs by someone who can only
  open it.
- **Quantum computers, for classic public keys.** An archive is safe from them only when every
  public-key slot is post-quantum: passwords, recovery keys and FIDO2 slots are; X25519, RSA and
  EC slots aren't. BeeZip shows which (decision 9).

## 3. The design

- **The file key.** 32 random bytes, made when an archive is first encrypted. Everything else is
  derived from it with HKDF-SHA256.
- **Slots.** Each slot holds the file key wrapped under a key that only its secret yields: a
  password through Argon2id, a recovery key, a private key, a FIDO2 credential. Any slot opens
  the archive. Each slot also stores a 32-byte commitment derived alongside its wrapping key, so
  a wrong secret is recognised before any decryption is tried and a crafted slot can't open
  under two different secrets.
- **The header MAC.** HMAC-SHA256 under a key derived from the file key and the archive id,
  over the whole start of the file: the 16-byte header and every slot. It is checked as soon as
  a slot opens, so the slots can't be changed, and it commits the archive to one file key, so
  two people who open it with different slots read the same archive.
- **The archive id** is 32 random bytes, new for every file written, including every edit.
- **Cells.** Each cell gets a random 32-byte id and its own key, derived from the file key and
  that id, and is sealed in 1 MiB segments, numbered, the last one flagged, as age and Tink do:
  nothing can be cut off or reordered, and each segment is checked before its bytes reach the
  decoder. The id lives only inside encrypted parts.
  Because a cell's key depends on its id and not on where it sits, an edit copies sealed cells
  into the new file as they are, which keeps M13's raw-copy edits (no decoding, no
  recompression). A cell that is re-packed gets a new id; an id never seals two different
  contents.
- **Records** (the recovery records before each cell, which hold paths) are sealed under the
  archive's record key with a random nonce in the cell block's head, and bound to their place
  in the file through the associated data, so a recovering reader with a way in can still list
  and extract what a cut-off archive holds, in order. (A nonce made from the place alone would
  repeat under one key in two copies of a file added to in place apart.)
- **The key id** is 16 random bytes in the key block, made with each file key and hashed into
  every slot's context, so a slot rebuilt for a new file key never reuses a wrap key.
- **The index** is sealed under a key from the file key and a random salt of its own, and
  bound to the header MAC. It lists every cell with its id, so cells can't be dropped, swapped
  or replaced by cells of an older version of the archive. A section of the encrypted index
  describes the slots (labels, public keys, certificates), so anyone who can open the archive
  can see who else can, and BeeZip can rebuild the slots when the key changes (§6).
- **What stays in the clear:** the header, the slots, the tags and lengths that frame each
  part (so each cell's stored size and its journal's, §1), and the footer. Every path, time,
  attribute, checksum and dedup decision, and each file's own size, is sealed.
- **Edits** (add, remove, rename, convert into an encrypted `.bhive`) keep the file key and the
  slots byte for byte, so an edit needs one way in and no device touches: a new archive id, new
  records, a new index, cells copied sealed.
- **Changing who can open it** (`archive.protect`, §8): adding a way needs the archive open;
  removing one drops its slot. Removing isn't revoking: someone who opened the archive before
  could have kept its file key. "New key" makes a new file key and key id, re-seals every cell
  and rebuilds every slot; a password is asked for again, a recovery key is new, and FIDO2
  slots need a touch each.
  - **Every slot is new:** each gets fresh randomness (a salt, an ephemeral key, an
    encapsulation, a secret), and no Argon2 output is ever kept for another archive. PRs A and
    F test that a new key changes every slot.
  - **The slot descriptions are claims, not facts:** anyone who has held the file key can
    rewrite section 5. "New key" shows each recipient it would rebuild, by fingerprint or
    certificate, and rebuilds only those the user confirms or already has as contacts (PRs F
    and G).
  - **Cells are copied as they are stored:** protect and New key never re-pack a cell (New key
    re-seals the same stored bytes), or the signatures would drop (PR A).

## 4. Ways to open an archive

| Slot | Who it's for | The secret | Where it lives | Post-quantum | Systems |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Password | you, or people you tell | a password | in someone's head | yes | all |
| 2 Recovery key | you, for when the rest fails | 48 digits (128 bits), as BitLocker's | on paper, in a safe place | yes | all |
| 3 Public key (classic) | someone who gave you an `age1…` key | an X25519 private key | their BeeZip, or any age tool | no | all |
| 4 Public key (post-quantum) | someone who gave you an `age1pq1…` key | an X-Wing (ML-KEM-768 + X25519) private key | their BeeZip, or age 1.3+ | yes | all |
| 5 RSA certificate | the holder of an RSA certificate (2048 bits or more) | its private key | Windows' store, a smart card or YubiKey, the TPM, a `.pfx` or PEM file, macOS Keychain | no | all; hardware on Windows |
| 6, 7 EC certificate, P-256 or P-384 | the holder of an EC certificate whose key can do ECDH | its private key | as above | no | as above |
| 8 Security key | the holder of a FIDO2 key (YubiKey, Titan…), or this PC's Windows Hello | the key's hmac-secret for a salt | the device | yes | Windows (webauthn.dll) |

- **"This PC"** is a slot 6 whose private key BeeZip makes in the TPM (ECDH P-256; unwraps in
  about 20 ms here), optionally with a PIN that the TPM protects against guessing. It never
  leaves the machine, so BeeZip requires another way that does (decision 11).
- **Windows Hello** is a slot 8 on the platform authenticator, where Windows supports
  hmac-secret for it (Windows 11 24H2 and 25H2 with the February 2026 update, by community
  reports; BeeZip probes rather than trusting a version). Elsewhere BeeZip offers "This PC"
  instead. It is machine-bound, under the same rule.
- **Certificates:** BeeZip encrypts to a certificate from Windows' stores (yours, and "Other
  People") or from a `.cer`, `.crt`, `.der` or `.pem` file, and opens with the matching private
  key wherever it is. It refuses keys that can't do the job, with the reason: ECDSA-only EC keys
  (the common kind: they can sign but not agree keys), RSA under 2048 bits, certificates whose
  key usage forbids key encipherment or agreement. An expired or untrusted certificate is
  allowed with a warning, since encryption doesn't depend on trust.
- **Tags.** A certificate, TPM or FIDO2 slot can't be tried without a PIN or a touch, so
  slots 5–7 carry a 4-byte tag computed from that slot's random part and the public key's hash:
  BeeZip finds the matching certificate before asking for anything, and someone without the
  certificate learns nothing from the tag. A slot 8 carries its credential id, which the device
  recognises. Public-key slots 3 and 4 are anonymous, as age's: a reader tries its keys, which
  costs a millisecond each.
- **age compatibility:** BeeZip's public keys and private keys are written in age's formats
  (`age1…`, `age1pq1…`, `AGE-SECRET-KEY-1…`, `AGE-SECRET-KEY-PQ-1…`), so a key made by
  `age-keygen` works in BeeZip and the other way round (checked in PR C against age's own
  test keys). The archives are not age files.

## 5. Passwords

- **Argon2id** (version 0x13), with its cost stored in the slot:
  - **Standard:** 1 GiB, 2 passes, 4 lanes on 4 threads. About 0.3 s here, about 0.55 s on a
    4-core laptop at half this machine's speed per core.
  - **Strong:** 2 GiB, 3 passes, 4 lanes. About 0.8 s here, 1.7 s on that laptop. It may page
    on an 8 GB machine, so BeeZip says how much memory it needs before it starts.
  - **Lanes must run on threads** (`argon2`'s `parallel` feature, on rayon): on one thread
    Standard takes 0.9 s here. PR A checks it; if the crate can't, BeeZip runs the lanes itself.
  - **Readers** refuse more than 4 GiB of memory, 16 passes or 16 lanes for a slot, and more
    than 4 password slots in a key block (each may cost a reader its Argon2), and ask before
    spending more than half the machine's memory.
- **Calibration:** none. The cost is a fixed level (decision 5), so an archive made on a fast
  desktop doesn't take a minute to open on a laptop.
- **NFC:** a password is normalised to Unicode NFC and hashed as UTF-8, so the same password
  typed on macOS and Windows opens the same archive.
- **Strength:** BeeZip estimates a typed password's guesses (length, character classes, the EFF
  wordlist and common-password patterns, keyboard runs) and warns under about 50 bits, the
  equivalent of four random words. It doesn't refuse: the user decides.
- **"Suggest one":** six words from the EFF large list (about 77 bits), separated by hyphens.
- **Mixing:** an archive with a password and someone else's public key or certificate gets a
  warning when it is made ("anyone who can open this could also make an archive your password
  opens"), as age's reason for not mixing them; BeeZip allows it, since one person often has
  both a password and a work certificate.

## 6. Keys and certificates

- **Your keys.** BeeZip makes post-quantum keys by default (decision 9) and imports age's. A
  private key is kept, per system:
  - **Windows:** a file under BeeZip's settings folder, sealed with DPAPI-NG for this Windows
    account (silent; under a millisecond).
  - **macOS:** the login Keychain, as a generic password.
  - **Linux:** a file readable only by you, as age and ssh keep theirs; optionally under a
    passphrase.
  Each can be exported as an `AGE-SECRET-KEY-PQ-1…` line for a backup, with a warning, and the
  public key copied to share.
- **Contacts:** a list in Settings of other people's public keys and certificates, by name, so
  they can be picked next time.
- **Certificates on Windows:** BeeZip lists those in "Personal" and "Other People" that can
  encrypt, shows their issuer and whether Windows trusts them, and uses CNG for the private
  key: software, smart card or TPM, never exporting it. A smart card's or TPM key's PIN dialog
  belongs to Windows and is parented to BeeZip's window. A `.pfx` is imported in memory, never
  into the store.
- **macOS:** Keychain identities and `.p12` files through Security.framework. **Linux:** PEM
  and PKCS#8 key files. Smart cards and security keys on macOS and Linux are §12.
- **Key derivation for EC slots** is HKDF with its first step done as HMAC-SHA256 keyed by the
  salt over the shared secret (big-endian): Windows computes exactly that with
  `BCRYPT_KDF_HMAC`, which smart-card drivers support, for software, TPM and card keys alike;
  everywhere else it is plain HKDF.
- **TPM keys** are made with no certificate, named by BeeZip, in the Platform Crypto Provider.
  RSA-2048 and ECDH P-256 are what TPMs promise; BeeZip uses P-256 (made in about 0.1 s, against
  1–2 s for RSA). An RSA-OAEP label must be empty, which TPMs require.

## 7. The format on disk

`docs/bhive.md` gains §12, written in PR 0 before the code (§10), with test vectors for every
slot kind and a small encrypted archive byte by byte. In outline:

```
header            16 bytes; features bit 0 set
key block         'BHKY', suite, archive id, key id, slots, header MAC
cell blocks       'BHCE', two lengths, the record's nonce, the sealed record, the sealed cell
index block       'BHIE', length, salt, the sealed index
footer            16 bytes, as in version 1
```

| Part | Clear | Sealed |
|---|---|---|
| Key block | tag, suite (1), archive id (32), key id (16), slot count and slots, MAC (32) | — |
| A slot | kind, length; kind-specific public part (Argon2 cost and salt, an ephemeral key or KEM ciphertext, a tag, FIDO2 credential id and relying party); commitment (32) | the file key (32 + 16) |
| Cell block | tag, sealed record length, sealed cell length, the record's nonce (12) | record: cell id, version 1's 48-byte record head, journal; cell: its stored bytes |
| Index block | tag, length, salt (32) | version 1's index head and index, with a cell id in each cell-table row, and a section describing the slots |

- **Keys** (all HKDF-SHA256 from the file key): the header MAC key with the archive id as salt;
  the record key with the archive id; each cell's key with its id; the index key with the
  index's salt. A record's nonce is its place in the file. Cells and the index are sealed in
  1 MiB segments whose nonce is the segment's number and a last-segment flag; each of their
  keys seals one cell or one index.
- **Associated data** binds each frame's clear fields to its contents, and the index to the
  header MAC.
- **A reader checks,** in addition to version 1's rules: the suite; slot lengths and counts
  (at most 64 slots, 64 KiB of slots); Argon2 limits; the MAC before anything else is used;
  each AEAD before decompressing; that every cell in the index exists with its id.
- **Recovery** works as in version 1, frame by frame, given a way in.
- **Signatures** (§11) are two index sections, the same in encrypted and unencrypted archives;
  in an encrypted one they are sealed with the rest of the index.
- **Cost on disk:** about 130 bytes a slot (a post-quantum one about 1,230); about 60 bytes a
  cell, 16 bytes per MiB sealed, and 32 bytes per cell in the index: 0.0015 % of a Fast archive.

## 8. How it fits

- **Core:** `format/bhive/crypt/` holds the envelope (slots, MAC, sealing) and one module per
  slot kind's pure-Rust half. A `KeySource` trait is how a slot gets its secret: passwords and
  recovery keys come through `Ctx` as passwords do today; keys and devices come from sources
  the commands layer supplies. The reader, writer, edits (`copy_from`) and recovery learn the
  sealed frames. `archive.info` reports the protection.
- **A new crate, `beezip-keys`,** holds the system key stores, so `unsafe` stays out of core:
  CNG (certificate stores, smart cards, TPM keys), DPAPI-NG, webauthn.dll on Windows; Keychain on
  macOS; key files everywhere. About six RAII wrappers cover CNG's handles.
- **Commands:**
  - `archive.create`, `archive.add` and `archive.convert` take a `protection` for `.bhive`:
    `password`, `recovery_key` (made and returned once), `recipients` (public keys),
    `certificates` (store thumbprints or files), `this_pc` (with an optional PIN),
    `security_keys`, `strength` (standard or strong).
  - `archive.protection` lists the ways in, with labels when the archive is open, and its
    post-quantum status; `archive.protect` adds and removes ways and makes a new key.

## 9. Tests

- **Test vectors:** the spec's vectors for every slot kind and the envelope, checked by Rust and
  by the Python reader; X-Wing against the draft's vectors, ML-KEM against Wycheproof.
- **Fixtures:** an encrypted archive per slot kind and level, from `cargo xtask gen-fixtures`,
  with the test keys committed beside them and marked as test keys.
- **Round trips:** the M13 proptest, encrypted, at every level, with mixed slots; every slot
  kind opens it, a wrong secret doesn't.
- **Malicious:** a changed byte in each part; slots edited, added, removed; cells swapped,
  dropped, duplicated or taken from an older version; truncated at every part; Argon2 costs
  over the limits; 2^32 slots; an index that names a missing cell.
- **Fuzzing:** the key block parser, and a whole archive under a fixed test key, with AEAD
  checks passing under `cfg(fuzzing)` as checksums do today (M13 §8).
- **Edits:** the edit suite over encrypted archives; sealed cells copied byte for byte; a new
  key re-seals everything.
- **Signing:** a signature survives a way in added or removed, a new key, and encrypting; it
  fails on any changed entry, cell or covered section, and on a signature moved from another
  archive; each algorithm against its vectors (RFC 8032 for Ed25519, NIST's ACVP for ML-DSA-65,
  Wycheproof for RSA-PSS and ECDSA); an unencrypted signed archive is read by 0.8.4's code
  (it skips the new sections) with the same result.
- **Windows:** CNG tests make throwaway software keys (and TPM keys where a TPM exists, which
  CI runners lack) and delete them.
- **Python:** the reader opens every fixture with every kind a key file can open, in CI on all
  three systems.

## 11. Signing

Added at the user's request when they approved the plan. A signature proves who made an
archive's contents and that nothing in them has changed since. It works on encrypted and
unencrypted `.bhive` alike, and it answers §1's "forgery by someone who can open it". The
details go into `docs/bhive.md` §13 in PR 0.

- **What is signed: the contents, not the layout.** A *manifest*, SHA-256 over: every cell's raw
  length, coder, parameter, checksum and the SHA-256 of its stored bytes (before any sealing),
  in number order; the index's entries exactly as encoded; and the index sections that describe
  contents (the spec marks which by id, so a section added later is covered or not by rule).
  Where cells lie, their ids, the sealing and the ways in are left out. So:
  - adding or removing a way in, "New key", and converting a signed archive into an encrypted
    one keep its signatures;
  - an edit that changes the contents (add, remove, rename) drops them. BeeZip says so first and
    offers to sign again when the signer's key is on this machine.
- **The signed message** is the manifest plus the algorithm, the signer's public key or
  certificate, and the time the signer claims, under a BeeZip-only prefix. A signature can't be
  moved to another archive, or to another signer.
- **Where:** two new index sections: each cell's SHA-256 (32 bytes a cell), and the signatures
  (any number: several people can sign one archive). BeeZip 0.8 skips sections it doesn't know
  (bhive.md §6), so it reads a signed unencrypted archive as before and ignores the signature.
  In an encrypted archive both are sealed with the index, so only someone who can open it sees
  who signed it.
- **Checking:** opening an archive checks its signatures against the index, in milliseconds.
  Each cell is checked against its digest as it is read, so extracting one file doesn't read
  the rest, and Test checks every cell. A cell that doesn't match fails like a cell with a bad
  checksum: its files are lost, the rest are read, and the archive is shown as changed.
- **What a signature covers is the index it is in:** an archive cut back to an earlier footer
  (§1, "Rollback") carries that index's signatures, and an archive recovered from its records
  is reported unsigned.
- **Limits a reader keeps** (bhive.md §13.3, §13.4): at most 16 signatures and 1 MiB of them, 8
  certificates a signer, RSA keys of 2,048 to 8,192 bits with an odd exponent below 2^32, and a
  key that matches its algorithm. A malformed section 2 or 3, or an unknown critical (odd)
  signed attribute, makes those signatures "couldn't check"; it never refuses the file.
- **Who can sign:**
  - **Your BeeZip signing key:** a post-quantum hybrid, ML-DSA-65 and Ed25519, both of which
    must verify, as X-Wing pairs ML-KEM with X25519. Ed25519 through `ring`; ML-DSA through
    `ml-dsa` (§2). Stored as your other private keys are (decision 14), shared as a `bzsig1…`
    string of about 3,200 characters, and shown with a 16-character fingerprint that can be
    read out over the phone.
  - **A certificate:** RSA-PSS with SHA-256 (PKCS #1 v1.5 where a key can't do PSS), ECDSA
    P-256 and P-384, including the ECDSA certificates that can't encrypt (§4). Through CNG on
    Windows (software, smart card or TPM; the PIN dialog is Windows'), Keychain on macOS, key
    files everywhere. The signature carries the certificate and the intermediate certificates
    the signer's machine has.
- **What BeeZip shows** once an archive is open:
  - **"Signed by <name>"** when the signature verifies and the signer is known: your own key, a
    contact, or a certificate that may sign (its key usage allows signatures) and whose chain
    the system trusts. That means Windows' chain engine, with its revocation check, or macOS's
    trust evaluation. Linux has no system trust for signing, so there a certificate is known only
    when it is a contact.
  - **"Signed by an unknown signer"** with the fingerprint, or the certificate's subject and
    issuer, and "Add to contacts".
  - **"Changed after it was signed"** in red when a signature fails or a cell doesn't match its
    digest. The archive still opens; the desktop asks before extracting, and the CLI warns.
  - **An expired or revoked certificate** is shown as such. The signing time is claimed by the
    signer, not proved; trusted timestamps are §12 (decision 28).
- **Commands:** `signing` on create, add and convert (your key, or a certificate);
  `archive.sign` adds or removes signatures on an existing archive, hashing its cells once if
  it has no digests yet; `archive.info` and `archive.test` report signatures and their status.
- **CLI:** `--sign <key|thumbprint|file>`; `beezip sign` and `beezip verify`; `--require-signer
  <contact|key|certificate file>` on extract and test refuses an archive that isn't signed,
  validly, by that signer, for scripts. It matches the whole SHA-256 of the signer bytes; the
  16-character fingerprint is for people to compare, never for matching.
- **MCP:** shows and checks signatures and can require a signer, but never signs: a signature
  says that you made the archive (decision 27).
- **Desktop:** a signature line at the top of the archive window, with details (the signer, the
  certificate in Windows' own viewer, the claimed time); "Sign with" in Create, Add and Convert;
  a Sign command; your signing key and contacts' keys in Settings → Keys.
- **Python reader:** checks signatures and digests (Ed25519 and ML-DSA-65 in pure Python, about
  300 lines with SHAKE from `hashlib`; RSA and ECDSA with the code it already has for slots).
  It shows certificates but doesn't check their chains.
- **Cost:** only when signing or checking, SHA-256 over the stored bytes (SHA-NI here).
  Estimated at 1–4 % at Fast and Balanced on source code and more on cells stored as they are;
  PR H measures it. A signature with your key is about 5.4 KB (public key 1,984 bytes, signature
  3,373); with a certificate, the certificates plus 64–512 bytes.

## 14. Decisions

Each is a recommendation, followed by its alternative. **Decisions 1–21 were approved by the
user on 2026-11-25** ("Approve all"), with decision 17 then changed at their request. Decisions
22–29 came with that change and with "I don't have a hardware key"; they follow the same
approach and can still be changed before PR 0 is merged.

1. **Strong encryption only in `.bhive`;** zip and 7z keep theirs as 0.8.4 left them, with a
   line in the dialog pointing to `.bhive`. *Alternative:* also offer zip's AES with a warning
   only (no pointer), or remove zip's ZipCrypto from the dialog.
2. **A feature bit of version 1, not a version 2.** BeeZip 0.8 refuses an encrypted `.bhive`
   ("unknown feature"); 0.9 says "made by a newer BeeZip" for features it doesn't know.
   *Alternative:* version 2, which every 0.8 also refuses, as "unknown version".
3. **AES-256-GCM through `ring`, with HKDF-SHA256 and HMAC-SHA256** (suite 1). A quarter of
   ChaCha20-Poly1305's CPU here, and nearly every x64 PC of the last decade and every ARM64 has
   AES instructions (ring falls back to constant-time code without them). AES-GCM, HKDF, HMAC, P-256, P-384, RSA-OAEP and ML-KEM are all
   FIPS-approved, which matters to some who use certificates. Per-cell keys and counted nonces
   keep AES-GCM's nonce limits far away. The suite byte leaves room for another.
   *Alternative:* ChaCha20-Poly1305 (constant-time without AES instructions; 1.4× faster in the
   Python reader; 4× the CPU here), or RustCrypto's `aes-gcm` instead of ring (pure Rust;
   unmeasured, likely slower).
4. **A key per cell from a random id; cells sealed in 1 MiB segments** with a counter and a
   last-segment flag. Sealing stays in the CPU's cache, where whole cells cost 27–35 % on
   stored cells; each piece is checked before decoding; the Python reader holds 1 MiB at a
   time. Sealed cells copy as they are on edits. *Alternative:* each cell one AEAD message:
   simpler, 16 bytes less per MiB, slower on stored cells.
5. **Argon2id at two fixed levels:** Standard 1 GiB, 2 passes, 4 lanes (about 0.3 s here,
   0.55 s on a laptop); Strong 2 GiB, 3 passes, 4 lanes (0.8 s and 1.7 s). *Alternatives:*
   calibrate to about one second on the machine that makes the archive (as LUKS2 and KeePassXC
   do), which can make it slow to open on a weaker one; or 512 MiB, 2 passes as Standard, kinder
   to 4 GB machines and two to four times cheaper to attack.
6. **The recovery key is 48 digits in 8 groups of 6, as BitLocker's** (128 bits, each group a
   multiple of 11 so a typo shows). *Alternative:* 26 letters and digits (base32, 128 bits), or
   longer for 160 or 256 bits.
7. **Slots hold a commitment and are copied unchanged on edits;** edits keep the file key, and
   "New key" re-seals and rebuilds slots from the descriptions in the encrypted index.
   *Alternative:* a new file key on every edit, which needs every device touched and every
   recipient's key each time.
8. **X-Wing for post-quantum keys, built from `ml-kem` and `x25519-dalek`,** checked against
   the draft's vectors; keys in age's formats. *Alternative:* the `x-wing` crate (draft
   version unclear), or ML-KEM-768 and X25519 concatenated with our own combiner.
9. **New keys are post-quantum by default;** classic `age1…` keys are accepted. BeeZip shows
   whether an archive resists quantum computers and doesn't refuse mixing (certificates are
   all classic). *Alternative:* as age, refuse to mix post-quantum and classic slots.
10. **Certificates: RSA-OAEP-SHA256 (2048 bits or more) and ECDH P-256 and P-384, found in
    Windows' stores or files, opened through CNG on Windows** (software, smart card, TPM),
    Keychain on macOS, key files on Linux. ECDSA-only keys are refused with the reason.
    *Alternative:* RSA only (most user certificates), leaving EC to a later version.
11. **"This PC" (TPM) and Windows Hello always come with a way that leaves the machine;**
    BeeZip won't make an archive whose only ways are bound to one PC. *Alternative:* allow it
    with a warning.
12. **Mixing a password with other people's keys is allowed with a warning** (§5).
    *Alternative:* refuse it, as age does.
13. **Password strength is BeeZip's own estimate,** with a warning under about 50 bits and no
    refusal; "Suggest one" gives six EFF words. *Alternative:* `zxcvbn` (better estimates, 30
    crates and 436 KB), or a refusal below a minimum.
14. **Your private keys are sealed by the system** (DPAPI-NG, Keychain; a 0600 file on Linux)
    and exportable in age's format. *Alternative:* always under a passphrase of their own, or
    in Windows' certificate store as non-exportable CNG keys.
15. **MCP:** passwords, recovery keys, your stored keys by name, recipients and certificates to
    encrypt to; no devices and no new recovery keys. *Alternative:* allow devices (a prompt
    would appear on the desktop for a request made by a model).
16. **The Python reader opens every slot a key file can** (password, recovery key, both public
    keys, RSA and EC from PEM or PKCS#8 files, plain or under a password), not `.pfx`.
    *Alternative:* add modern `.pfx` (about 130 more lines).
17. **Signing is in M14** (§11, PR H). *Changed 2026-11-25 at the user's request* ("Can 17 be
    done now too?"); it first said signing would wait for a later milestone.
18. **PRs 0 and A–H are committed, pushed and merged when green without asking,** stopping
    before the 0.9.0 release, as for M8–M13. *Alternative:* stop for the user's word before
    each merge.
19. **Wave 1 runs as a workflow of eight parallel agents** (seven when approved, H added with
    signing), one per PR in its own worktree, which uses tokens in a burst. *Alternative:* two
    or three at a time, or one after another.
20. **What needs hands is one sitting with the user** after wave 1 (§10). *Alternative:* a
    short check with the user as each device PR lands.
21. **Ship in 0.9.0,** with encryption marked as part of `.bhive` version 1 and frozen.
    *Alternative:* ship it first as a preview that BeeZip promises to keep reading but may stop
    writing.
22. **Signatures cover the contents, not the layout** (§11): changing the ways in, a new key and
    encrypting keep them; any change to the contents drops them. *Alternative:* sign the whole
    index and the slots ("exactly this file"), which is simpler but needs the signer again
    whenever someone is given a way in.
23. **Signatures live in index sections,** so BeeZip 0.8 still reads a signed unencrypted
    archive (ignoring the signature), and in an encrypted archive the signer is hidden from
    anyone who can't open it. *Alternative:* a feature bit, so 0.8 refuses signed archives as
    it refuses encrypted ones.
24. **Your signing key is a post-quantum hybrid, ML-DSA-65 with Ed25519,** both checked, as
    decision 9 does for encryption. *Alternative:* Ed25519 alone (a 56-character key, a 64-byte
    signature, no quantum resistance), or minisign's format so minisign can check BeeZip's
    signatures.
25. **Certificates sign with RSA-PSS (PKCS #1 v1.5 where a key can't do PSS) and ECDSA P-256 and
    P-384; trust is the system's:** Windows' chain engine with its revocation check (which may
    go online to the certificate's issuer), macOS's trust evaluation, and on Linux only your
    contacts. *Alternative:* never go online (revocation only from Windows' cache), or ship
    Mozilla's root list for Linux (made for websites, not for signing).
26. **A signature that fails warns, it doesn't block:** the archive opens marked "changed after
    it was signed", the desktop asks before extracting, the CLI warns; `--require-signer`
    refuses. *Alternative:* refuse to extract an archive whose signature fails.
27. **The MCP server shows and checks signatures, and can require a signer, but never signs.**
    *Alternative:* let it sign with your stored keys by name, as it may open with them.
28. **Trusted timestamps (RFC 3161) wait;** the signature keeps room for one. *Alternative:* add
    them now on Windows (`CryptRetrieveTimeStamp`, which sends the archive's digest to a
    timestamp server), and later elsewhere.
29. **Security keys and smart cards are built without one here:** the FIDO2 path is tried with
    Windows Hello through the same webauthn.dll call, and a smart card's through the generic
    CNG path (and a TPM virtual smart card, if you make one). They ship in 0.9.0, marked in
    the docs as not yet tried on a physical device. *Alternative:* leave "Security key" out of
    0.9.0 until someone tries one.

## 16. The 7z fix (0.8.4)

Found while answering the question, and fixed before this plan: BeeZip's 7z archives stretched
a password through 2^8 SHA-256 rounds, not 7-Zip's 2^19, so each guess cost 2,048 times less,
and every block and the header shared one IV. Both came from sevenz-rust2 0.25.0's defaults
(`docs/UPSTREAM.md`). PR #75 sets 7-Zip's rounds and a fresh IV per block; archives made before
keep the weak setting until they are made again.
